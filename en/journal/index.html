Development log

What's done, with the date

An entry goes here only once it has been measured on the bench, on a real controller. Entries marked “Design” are checked in the design files, until the prototype tests. Problems found are posted here too. Newest first.

  1. October 31, 2026

    First test on the bike: one part with the wrong value, found and fixed

    On the bike, the first circuit boards lost their connection as soon as the bike was switched on, and two modules were damaged. Powered on the bench without the bike's battery, they worked.

    The cause is measured: in the parts list, one resistor in the circuit that watches the bike's power switch had the wrong part number, 2.4 Ω instead of 470 kΩ (about 3 Ω measured on an unused board). When the bike switched on, it let the battery voltage through to the microcontroller. On the remaining module, the resistor is replaced and the switch-on is softened: the bike now turns on over a fraction of a second instead of all at once. Revision 0.2 fixes the parts list at the source.

  2. October 30, 2026

    Circuit board, revision 0.2: the version meant for sale Design

    Revision 0.2 fixes the parts list and separates the bike's power from the module's own supply: switching the bike on can no longer starve the module. The soft switch-on is built in, surge protection is tightened, and the 3.3 V regulator tolerates a much higher input voltage. It adds the R7 enclosure's status lights.

    The schematic and the board layout match part for part, and every part is in stock at the manufacturer. Next: order the prototypes and test them on the bike. The results will be posted here.

  3. October 24, 2026

    R7 enclosure: slimmer, stronger Design

    The enclosure is redesigned and checked in the design files: clearance around the board on every side, screw depths, seal squeeze, the cable-gland thread and pad compression. It measures 62 × 85 × 29 mm, 41% thinner than the first printed prototype (49 mm), with 60% less material.

    One-piece shell with a continuous seal, board held without screws, two screws instead of eight, a tool-free clip for 22.2 to 35 mm bars, and three status lights: module powered, phone connected, latch state. Next: print the prototypes and test sealing, mounting and cold. The results will be posted here.

  4. October 14, 2026

    First circuit boards received

    Kurant's first factory-assembled circuit boards (revision 0.1) have arrived. The firmware installed on the first try, through the programming connector.

  5. October 10, 2026

    Power-on, assist and a dropped phone: bench-tested

    The module turns the bike on when the app asks and changes the assist level from the phone. When the phone disconnects, assist keeps the level you chose.

  6. October 6, 2026

    A motor simulator for the next profile

    To capture a second controller's profile on the bench without riding, a small circuit simulates the motor's sensors, which keeps the controller from throwing its error code.

  7. October 2, 2026

    A second bike on the bench

    An adapter cable connects a second bike (48 V) to the test bench, and its whole connector is mapped. It's the first step toward the next profile.

  8. October 1, 2026

    Final circuit schematic, revision 0.1

    The board uses an already-certified ESP32-C3 wireless module. The latch that keeps the bike on is hardened with pull-down resistors and a power-on reset.

  9. September 29, 2026

    Reference captured from a KT-LCD5 display

    The firmware's reference frame is captured again from a KT-LCD5 display, with all of its values.

  10. September 25, 2026

    Signed over-the-air updates: code written

    The module only accepts signed firmware, and the private key stays offline. Testing on the final hardware is next.

  11. September 19, 2026

    Kurant replaces the display

    With no stock display, the module takes over the KT controller in 329 ms, at 9600 baud, with 3 of 3 checksums valid. First command verified end to end: the headlight turns on (frame byte 1: 01 → 81; headlight output from 0 V to battery voltage). The stock display, plugged back in afterwards, kept every setting.

  12. September 2026

    The power latch holds

    The circuit that keeps the bike on works with the 48 V battery and survives a reset, a USB unplug and removing the microcontroller. An intermittent failure was found with the logic analyzer (too little base current, 2.6 mA) and fixed.

  13. September 2026

    Listening to the KT controller

    The module reads the controller's and the display's frames at 9600 baud, with valid checksums. Brake state is decoded.
